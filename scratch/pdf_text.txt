ABSTRACT
Ankle fractures are common musculoskeletal injuries and may require open reduction and
internal fixation (ORIF), followed by a rehabilitation period in which restoration of ankle
movement, functional control, and postural stability is important. Population-based evidence
reports an ankle-fracture incidence of 168.7 per 100,000 people per year in one large decadelong cohort, while global burden estimates identify lower-leg fractures including ankle
fractures among the most common and burdensome fracture categories [1], [2]. Conventional
rehabilitation assessment relies substantially on clinical observation and periodic outcome
measures, while wearable-sensor research shows promise for objective movement assessment
but also reports methodological, validation, and clinical-translation limitations [3], [4], [5],
[6].
This individual component investigates whether a two-IMU ankle sensing configuration can
provide objective, repeatable measures of ankle kinematics and postural stability and use
those measures to characterize longitudinal recovery after ankle-fracture surgery. The
proposed component will synchronize and calibrate one IMU on the lower leg/tibia segment
and one on the foot, estimate relative segment orientation, derive exercise and stability
features, and combine repeated measurements into an Ankle Rehabilitation Progress Index
(ARPI). Temporal sequence learning will be investigated for exercise recognition, while
regression/forecasting model types will be evaluated for continuous recovery-state and
clinician-defined milestone estimation. The research will follow a Design Science Researchoriented, iterative experimental approach with benchmark comparison, controlled validation,
and usability assessment. The component forms the ankle-rehabilitation measurement and
prediction layer of the complete AKIRA solution; it does not prescribe treatment or replace
clinician decisions.
Keywords: ankle fracture ORIF; inertial measurement unit; ankle kinematics; postural
stability; longitudinal rehabilitation

1. Introduction
1.1. Background and Context
Ankle fractures are common musculoskeletal injuries that can require a prolonged
rehabilitation period, particularly when surgical treatment is necessary. In cases where
fracture alignment or stability requires surgical intervention, open reduction and internal
fixation (ORIF) is used to restore and maintain the appropriate anatomical position of the
fractured bones. A population-based study of 9,767 ankle fractures reported a mean
annual incidence of 168.7 per 100,000 people, with falls and sports identified among the
major injury mechanisms [1]. At the global level, the Global Burden of Disease analysis
estimated 178 million new fractures in 2019 and identified lower-leg fractures, including
ankle-related fractures, as an important contributor to the overall fracture burden [2].
These findings demonstrate the scale of ankle and lower-limb fracture recovery and
support the investigation of practical technologies for monitoring rehabilitation.
Following ORIF, rehabilitation progresses according to the patient's clinical condition and
recovery. Rehabilitation may involve gradual restoration of ankle movement,
strengthening, balance, and functional activities, while progression is influenced by
factors such as fixation stability, healing status, range of motion, strength, functional
ability, and clinician assessment. Evidence from rehabilitation and postoperative weightbearing research indicates that outcomes can vary according to clinical context, fixation
stability, patient characteristics, and rehabilitation protocols [7] - [8]. Therefore, the
proposed system is designed to support objective measurement and follow-up rather than
establish a universal rehabilitation schedule. It will not prescribe exercises, modify
weight-bearing restrictions, or override clinical instructions.
The specific problem addressed by this component is the difficulty of obtaining low-cost,
repeatable, and objective measurements of ankle exercise performance and postural
stability across repeated rehabilitation sessions. Clinical observation and functional
assessment remain important, but assessments are generally performed at specific clinical
visits and may provide only intermittent information about movement performance.
Wearable inertial sensing provides an opportunity to capture movement data during
rehabilitation activities and support repeated assessment outside conventional assessment
intervals. However, previous research has identified important methodological
considerations involving sensor placement, sensor-to-segment calibration, kinematic
validity, and analytical assumptions [3] - [6]. These limitations indicate that a reliable
sensing approach requires explicit calibration, synchronized measurements, and
appropriate validation.

For this component, two synchronized inertial measurement units (IMUs) will be
positioned on the lower leg/tibia segment and the foot segment. The relative orientation
between these two segments will be used to estimate ankle movement, with particular
emphasis on dorsiflexion and plantarflexion. The proposed exercise set includes ankle
pumps, ankle circles, ankle alphabet, dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, seated heel raises,
standing heel raises, and single-leg balance. Movement-related measures such as ankle
angle, range of motion, movement velocity, repetitions, trajectory characteristics, and
movement variability will be considered together with postural-stability measures such as
sway and stability-related motion variation. These measurements will be evaluated across
repeated rehabilitation sessions and summarized using the proposed Ankle Rehabilitation
Progress Index (ARPI) to represent changes in rehabilitation performance over time.
The proposed component therefore focuses on developing and evaluating a wearablesensor-based approach for ankle exercise recognition, movement assessment, posturalstability assessment, and longitudinal rehabilitation monitoring following ankle-fracture
ORIF. The component contributes to the wider AKIRA solution through its ankle-specific
rehabilitation measurements while maintaining a clear responsibility boundary from the
other group components. The research is aligned with Sustainable Development Goal 3
(Good Health and Well-Being) because it investigates technology-assisted monitoring that
can support rehabilitation assessment and follow-up.

Critical Synthesis. The reviewed evidence supports the feasibility of wearable inertial
sensing for lower-limb movement assessment but also demonstrates that reliable
rehabilitation monitoring requires more than exercise classification. Sensor placement and
calibration can directly influence kinematic validity, while rehabilitation outcomes may vary
according to clinical condition, fixation stability, patient characteristics, and rehabilitation
protocol [3]. Furthermore, the limited reporting of repeatability, measurement error, and
clinically meaningful change in foot and ankle wearable-sensor studies highlights the need
for systematic validation across repeated measurements [8]. Therefore, this research will
investigate a calibrated two-IMU ankle-sensing approach that combines movement and
postural-stability measures and evaluates their consistency across repeated rehabilitation
sessions.
1.2.1. Existing Solutions and Their Limitations
For comparison, the existing approaches considered in this proposal fall into four
broad groups: clinical visual assessment, laboratory motion capture, camera or depthbased exercise analysis, and wearable inertial sensing. Clinical assessment is
accessible and clinically meaningful, but it usually provides measurements only
during visits and can depend on the assessor. Motion-capture systems can offer
detailed reference measurements, although they normally need specialized equipment
and a controlled setting. Camera-based methods avoid body-worn sensors, but field of
view, occlusion, lighting, and privacy can become issues. IMUs are more portable and
simpler to deploy, but their accuracy depends strongly on placement, calibration,
signal processing, and validation [3] - [6].
Commercial rehabilitation platforms and general activity wearables show that guided
exercise and wearable monitoring are feasible. However, the evidence reviewed for
15

this proposal does not demonstrate a validated longitudinal system specifically for
post-ORIF ankle rehabilitation that brings together two-segment ankle kinematics,
exercise performance, postural stability, and clinician-defined recovery milestones.
The aim here is therefore not to claim that the proposed system is better than a
particular commercial product. Instead, it addresses the limitations identified in the
reviewed research.

1.3. Research Gap
The reviewed evidence demonstrates the potential of wearable IMUs for lower-limb
movement assessment, while also identifying important limitations related to sensor
calibration, kinematic validation, repeatability, and clinical translation [3] - [6]. Recent
evidence specific to foot and ankle assessment remains relatively small and
heterogeneous, with limited and inconsistent reporting of measurement error, reliability,
and clinically meaningful change [8]. Therefore, the research gap addressed by this
component is the lack of a validated ankle-specific longitudinal framework that combines
two-IMU kinematic assessment with postural-stability measures to objectively describe
rehabilitation progress over repeated sessions and estimate progress toward cliniciandefined rehabilitation milestones following ankle-fracture ORIF.

3. Methodology
3.1. Research Methodology/ Research Design
This study will follow a Design Science Research-oriented approach supported by
iterative experimental evaluation. The central research question for this component is
whether two wearable IMUs can provide ankle movement and postural-stability
measurements that are sufficiently accurate, repeatable, and interpretable to support
longitudinal rehabilitation assessment following ankle-fracture ORIF.
The research therefore requires both the development and evaluation of a working
artifact. The study will begin by defining the rehabilitation-monitoring problem based on
the identified evidence and research gap. This will be followed by the design of the twoIMU sensing configuration, sensor-placement procedure, calibration process, and dataprocessing pipeline. Pilot data will then be collected to evaluate the feasibility and quality
of the measurement procedure. The processing pipeline will be refined based on the pilot
findings before exercise-recognition, movement-feature, and stability-feature evaluation.

The validated measurements will subsequently be used to construct session-level
rehabilitation summaries and the proposed Ankle Rehabilitation Progress Index
(ARPI). Repeated-session measurements will then be analysed to represent changes in
rehabilitation status and support estimation of progress toward predefined, cliniciandefined rehabilitation milestones. Finally, the developed component will be evaluated
using quantitative performance measures and usability feedback. This iterative process
allows design decisions to be evaluated experimentally rather than assuming that a
sensing configuration or analytical approach will be suitable without validation.

3.2. Data/ Participants/ Experimental Inputs
Three main sources of data are planned for this study. The first source will be ankle
exercise and balance data collected using the two-IMU prototype. The second source
will consist of public or literature-derived non-clinical datasets, which may be used
for pipeline benchmarking when their sensor configuration, movement tasks, and data
characteristics are sufficiently compatible with the proposed approach. The third source
will be longitudinal post-ORIF rehabilitation data collected specifically for this
research. Collection of post-ORIF data will begin only after the necessary institutional
and ethical approvals have been obtained. Public datasets will not be treated as
representative of post-ORIF rehabilitation unless their documented population, tasks, and
context support such an interpretation.
For initial engineering validation, a pilot sample of approximately 20–30 adults is being
considered. A smaller healthy participant group may also be used during early protocol
development and debugging of the sensing and processing pipeline. Participants with
post-ORIF rehabilitation experience will be recruited only after ethical approval and will
be assessed according to appropriate eligibility criteria, exercise restrictions, and
rehabilitation stage. The final sample size will be determined based on feasibility
findings, expected measurement variation, validation requirements, and guidance from
the supervisor and relevant ethics process.
During each data-collection session, the two IMUs will operate using synchronized
timestamps. Sensor placement, calibration information, participant identifier, and session
identifier will be recorded before measurement. Accelerometer and gyroscope streams
will be checked for missing or poor-quality samples and aligned temporally before further
processing. Filtering and signal-processing parameters will be selected based on the
characteristics identified during pilot testing rather than being fixed without empirical
justification.
Exercise recordings will be labelled according to the performed exercise and, where
applicable, repetition boundaries. The planned exercise set includes ankle pumps, ankle
circles, ankle alphabet, dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, seated heel raises, standing heel
raises, and single-leg balance. For balance activities, task duration and relevant trial
outcomes will also be recorded where appropriate.
Data preparation will include:
• missing-sample and signal-quality checks;
• timestamp alignment between the two IMUs;
• sensor-offset and sensor-to-segment calibration;
• orientation estimation;
• movement segmentation;
• exercise and repetition labelling;
• feature extraction; and
• preparation of session-level rehabilitation measurements.
Normalization will only be applied where there is a clear methodological justification.
Training, validation, and test datasets will be separated by participant, ensuring that
measurements from the same participant do not appear across different dataset splits. This
19

is intended to reduce the risk of participant-specific information influencing model
evaluation.

3.3. Measurement and Processing Pipeline
The proposed measurement and processing pipeline begins with the placement of two
synchronized IMUs on the lower leg/tibia segment and foot segment using a
documented placement procedure. The sensor streams are synchronized before analysis,
and calibration establishes the relationship between each sensor coordinate frame and its
corresponding anatomical segment.
The accelerometer and gyroscope measurements will then be processed using sensorfusion methods to estimate the orientation of each segment. The relative orientation
between the lower-leg and foot segments will subsequently be used to obtain ankle-angle
trajectories for the movement planes investigated in this study, with particular emphasis
on dorsiflexion and plantarflexion.
The processing pipeline will contain two complementary analytical branches. The first
branch will analyse dynamic movement data for exercise segmentation, exercise
recognition, repetition counting, and movement-quality assessment. The second
branch will analyse balance-task data for postural-stability assessment, including
measures related to sway and sensor-motion variation.
The resulting features will be validated before being aggregated into session-level
measurements. Where appropriate, measurements will be compared with the participant's
baseline to represent individual change rather than relying only on population-level
values. Repeated session summaries will then be used to calculate the proposed ARPI,
which will provide a structured representation of rehabilitation progress. These
longitudinal measurements will subsequently support recovery-state analysis and
estimation of progress toward clinician-defined rehabilitation milestones.

3.4. Baseline/ Benchmark
For ankle-angle estimation, the primary comparison will be made against a reference
measurement procedure selected during protocol finalization. Depending on the
facilities available, this reference may be a clinician-based measurement procedure or
laboratory-based reference kinematics. The purpose of this comparison is to determine
whether the two-IMU approach provides sufficiently accurate ankle-motion
measurements for the intended rehabilitation-monitoring application.
For exercise recognition, simple signal-feature and rule-based approaches will first be
established as baseline methods. These baselines will provide a reference against which
21

the performance of the proposed temporal analysis approach can be evaluated. For
recovery-state and longitudinal estimation, a basic regression or trajectory-based
approach using session-level rehabilitation features will be used as the initial
benchmark.
More complex analytical approaches will only be considered beneficial if they
demonstrate measurable improvement over the established baselines under the same
participant-independent evaluation procedure. This prevents model complexity from
being treated as an advantage without corresponding evidence of improved performance.
The exact reference measurement procedure will be confirmed following the pilot
feasibility assessment because access to a specific laboratory reference system cannot be
assumed at the proposal stage. If a laboratory-based reference system is unavailable, the
study will use a clearly documented reference protocol and explicitly report this limitation
when interpreting the ankle-kinematic validation results

3.6. Validation Strategy
Validation will be conducted at multiple levels to assess both the technical performance
and practical usefulness of the proposed ankle-rehabilitation component.
First, the sensor placement, calibration, and ankle-angle estimation procedures will be
evaluated against the selected reference measurement procedure. This will determine
whether the two-IMU configuration can produce sufficiently accurate ankle-kinematic
measurements for the intended application.
Second, exercise recognition and repetition analysis will be evaluated using
participant-independent data splits. Participants included in the test set will not appear in
the training data, reducing the possibility that participant-specific movement patterns
artificially improve the reported performance.
Third, postural-stability features will be evaluated for repeatability and agreement
across repeated trials and, where data are available, repeated sessions. This will determine
whether stability measurements remain sufficiently consistent to support longitudinal
monitoring.
Fourth, longitudinal recovery-state and milestone estimation will use time-ordered
evaluation. Later rehabilitation sessions will not be used to predict earlier sessions,
thereby maintaining the temporal direction of the recovery-analysis problem.
The contribution of different feature groups will also be examined by comparing:
1. kinematic features alone;
2. postural stability features alone; and
3. combined kinematic and stability features represented through the proposed ARPI.
Where the available sample size permits, confidence intervals and error distributions will
be reported alongside point estimates. Usability testing and stakeholder review will also
be incorporated into the evaluation to identify practical issues that may not be captured by
technical metrics.
Any clinical interpretation of the results will remain within the intended scope of the
research. The system will be presented as a rehabilitation-monitoring and decisionsupport technology, rather than as a diagnostic system or an autonomous treatment
system.

3.7. Development Approach
An iterative development approach will be used because several aspects of the
proposed component—including sensor placement, calibration, signal processing,
exercise analysis, and user interaction—may require refinement based on pilot findings.
Each development iteration will produce a working version of the relevant component,
evaluate it against the predefined criteria, document identified problems, and use the
results to guide the next iteration.
The development process will proceed through the following stages:
1. Prototype the sensing and synchronization layer
Implement the two-IMU data-acquisition process and establish reliable timestamp
synchronization between the lower-leg and foot sensors.
2. Validate calibration and basic ankle-angle estimation
Implement the sensor-to-segment calibration procedure and evaluate the resulting
ankle-angle measurements against the selected reference procedure.
3. Add exercise segmentation, recognition, and repetition analysis
Develop the time-series processing required to identify the proposed ankle
exercises, segment exercise repetitions, and calculate movement-related features.
4. Add postural-stability analysis and ARPI calculation
Implement stability-related feature extraction and combine validated movement
and stability measures into the proposed Ankle Rehabilitation Progress Index.
5. Evaluate longitudinal recovery-state and milestone estimation
Use repeated session-level measurements to investigate rehabilitation-state
changes and estimate progress toward clinician-defined milestones.
6. Conduct usability and integration testing, then freeze the evaluated version
Evaluate the component with intended users and relevant stakeholders, address
identified usability and integration issues, and establish the final version used for
the main evaluation.
This iterative sequence ensures that longitudinal analysis is performed only after the
underlying sensing, calibration, movement, and stability measurements have been
sufficiently evaluated.

6. Commercialization Plan
6.1. Target Market and Customer Persona
The initial target market is physiotherapy and orthopaedic rehabilitation services that
support patients after ankle-fracture surgery. Rehabilitation researchers and supervised
home-rehabilitation programmes are possible secondary users.
The main user considered for the proposed system is a physiotherapist or rehabilitation
professional who wants objective ankle movement and balance measurements between
routine clinical assessments without setting up a laboratory motion-capture environment.
From the patient side, the workflow is intended to remain simple and to operate within the
rehabilitation plan provided by the clinician.

6.2. Value Proposition
•
•
•
•
•

Portable two-IMU ankle measurement rather than laboratory-only motion capture.
Exercise-specific quantitative measures instead of only subjective completion
reporting.
Separate kinematic and postural-stability measures to avoid reducing rehabilitation to
a single movement score.
Longitudinal ARPI and milestone trajectories that summarize change across sessions.
Clinician-support positioning: monitoring and evidence generation without
autonomous treatment decisions.

6.3. Revenue Model
One possible commercial model is a clinic package containing the wearable sensor kit,
local analysis software, onboarding, and optional support or maintenance. A separate
research or institutional licence could also be considered for universities and
rehabilitation research groups. Pricing cannot be finalized at the proposal stage because it
will depend on prototype costs, validation results, regulatory requirements, support needs,
and feedback from potential users

6.4. Cost Estimation and Pricing Strategy
The budget shown in Section 7 should be treated as a development estimate for the
research prototype, not as the final cost of a commercial product. A realistic selling price
would need to include hardware, assembly, validation, support, replacement, and
compliance costs. At this stage, the intended approach is to consider clinic value while
keeping the hardware and software/support portions transparent rather than simply adding
a margin to the student prototype budget.

6.5. Competitive Advantage
The main potential point of differentiation is the combination of ankle-specific twosegment kinematics, postural stability, exercise recognition, repeatability assessment, and
longitudinal milestone tracking in one workflow. This remains a provisional research
proposition. A stronger competitive claim would require a separate market and competitor
study.

6.6. Intellectual Property Considerations
Possible intellectual-property outputs include the sensor-placement and calibration
procedure, the feature-engineering approach, the ARPI formulation, the longitudinal
analysis workflow, the software implementation, and the integration interfaces. Opensource libraries and datasets used during development will need to be checked for licence
compatibility. Any decision about patentability, ownership, or public release should be
discussed with SLIIT and the research supervisor.
